THE SIX BASIC TECHNIQUES · VERBATIM DEFINITIONS
1 · Maintaining the analytic frameworka whole range of procedural and stylistic factors, such as the analyst’s relative anonymity, maintaining neutrality and objectivity, the regularity and consistency of meetings, starting and ending the sessions on time, clarity on fees, and basic boundary issues(Corey, 2024, p. 81)
2 · Free associationclients are encouraged to say whatever comes to mind, regardless of how painful, silly, trivial, illogical, or irrelevant it may seem(Corey, 2024, p. 82)
3 · Interpretationthe analyst pointing out, explaining, and even teaching the client the meanings of behavior that is manifested in dreams, free association, resistances, defenses, and the therapeutic relationship itself(Corey, 2024, p. 82)
4 · Dream analysisroyal road to the unconscious(Corey, 2024, p. 83)
5 · Resistanceanything that works against the progress of therapy and prevents the client from producing previously unconscious material(Corey, 2024, p. 83)
6 · Transference (in the process)transference manifests itself in the therapeutic process when earlier relationships contribute to clients distorting the present with the therapist(Corey, 2024, p. 84)
TECHNIQUEKEY POINT
1 Analytic frameworkThe consistent framework is itself a therapeutic factor.
2 Free associationThe cardinal technique; identifies repressed material. Blockings or disruptions cue anxiety-arousing material. Slips of the tongue are analyzed.
3 InterpretationHelps the ego assimilate new material; speeds uncovering. Interpret what is close to conscious awareness; start at the surface.
4 Dream analysisLatent content (hidden, symbolic, unconscious motives) → dream work → manifest content (the dream as it appears). Asleep, the ego releases control over unconscious material.
5 ResistanceRespect it: it defends against anxiety. Do not strip all of it away; interpret the most obvious first. Examples: missing sessions, arriving late, refusing to pay, censoring thoughts, disrupting free association, refusing to report dreams.
6 TransferenceThe core of the process: here-and-now insight into the influence of the past. Freud first saw it as an impediment, then as what made cure possible.
7 ConfrontationMakes sure the client is aware of the action or experience being analyzed ("You seem to be feeling angry toward me"). A feedback procedure.
8 ClarificationSharper, more detailed feedback about the specific phenomenon; often blends with confrontation.
9 Working throughThe "last and longest step": aware of defenses and impulses, sees they are less dangerous than feared, uses more mature controls. Structural change.
10 Therapeutic immediacyHere-and-now talk about the client and counselor relationship; addresses transference and resistance; a corrective emotional experience.
11 JournalingFree association and dream exploration on paper: stream of consciousness, free writing, expressive writing.
12 Life and death instinctsSelf-destructive behavior as death-instinct energy turned inward (masochism, self-loathing) or outward (aggression, violence).
13 Expert skillsSilence ("shut up and listen"); interpret symptoms as a useful past response; frame interpretations as hunches; monitor countertransference.

Multicultural Perspective

IN ONE LINECan suit diverse clients if modified, but classical analysis carries Western, costly, long-term limits.
Can suit diverse clients if techniques are modified.

STRENGTHS

  • Can suit diverse clients if techniques are modified
  • Erikson's psychosocial approach applies across cultures
  • Training stresses the therapist's own therapy, exposing personal bias and countertransference

SHORTCOMINGS

  • Long-term personality reconstruction rather than problems of living; costly
  • Seen as based on upper- and middle-class Western values
  • Ambiguity may not fit clients who expect an active, structured therapist
  • Does not adequately address social, cultural, and political factors; may be unhelpful for low-income clients in crisis
  • Classical analysis not readily adaptable to other cultures or sexual minorities; most research used white clients

Contributions and Limitations

IN ONE LINEIts major contribution is the unconscious; its limits are time, cost, and limited evidence in classical form.
Major contribution: its emphasis on the unconscious.

CONTRIBUTIONS

  • Major contribution: emphasis on the unconscious; transference and countertransference
  • A conceptual framework for the origins and functions of symptoms
  • Helps understand resistances (canceling, leaving early), unfinished business, transference, overuse of ego defenses
  • A comprehensive theory with breadth and depth in personality development and coping

LIMITATIONS

  • Time, expense, few trained analysts; long time commitment
  • Many severely disturbed clients lack the needed ego strength
  • The anonymous role can be unduly restrictive outside classical analysis
  • Feminist criticism of the Oedipus and Electra complexes; psychosexual stages seen as mainly Eurocentric
  • In classical form, limited empirical support
